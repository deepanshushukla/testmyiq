The question "what's a good score" only makes sense once you know what
scale you're being compared against — a score of 115 means something very
different on a scale designed around 100 versus one designed around 500.

## The standard scale: mean 100, standard deviation 15

On the scale most clinical IQ tests use, scores are built to form a bell
curve centered at 100, with a standard deviation of 15. That means:

- About 68% of people fall between 85 and 115 ("average" range).
- About 95% fall between 70 and 130.
- A score above 130 (roughly the top 2%) is typically labeled
  "very superior" or "gifted" in classification tables; below 70 is
  typically flagged for further evaluation.

These bands only hold because the scale was deliberately normed to
produce that distribution — it's a property of the measurement, not a
law of nature about how intelligence is distributed.

## Why percentile matters more than the raw number

"120" is meaningless on its own — what matters is that, on a correctly
normed scale, it puts you roughly at the 91st percentile. Two different
tests can report the same number on different underlying scales and mean
completely different things, which is why comparing scores across test
providers without checking their methodology is close to meaningless.
