Abstract reasoning strips away language and numbers entirely. You're
shown shapes or symbols that change according to a hidden rule, and asked
to apply the same rule somewhere new.

## Why it's considered "culture-fair"

Verbal and numerical questions can be affected by vocabulary, education,
or native language — a question about "ratio" or an uncommon English word
measures something extra beyond reasoning ability for a non-native
speaker. Non-verbal, abstract reasoning tasks were specifically developed
to reduce that confound: Raven's Progressive Matrices (Raven, 1938), one
of the best-known tests of this kind, uses only shapes and patterns for
exactly this reason. "Culture-fair" doesn't mean perfectly free of any
bias — but it removes language as a confound more directly than a
vocabulary test can.

## The transformation rules to look for

Most abstract reasoning items use one or a small combination of these
rules applied consistently across a series:

- **Rotation** — a shape turns by a fixed angle each step.
- **Reflection** — a shape mirrors across an axis.
- **Addition/subtraction of an element** — a dot, line, or shape is added
  or removed each step.
- **Size or shading change** — a shape grows, shrinks, or its fill
  changes (solid → striped → outline).
- **Combination** — two of the above happen at once, which is what makes
  harder items harder: you have to track two independent changes, not
  just one.

## A worked approach

When you see a series of shapes, don't try to guess the answer first —
describe out loud (or in your head) exactly what changed between each
step. Once you've named the rule in words ("each shape loses one corner"),
applying it to a new shape becomes mechanical instead of a guess.
