Spatial reasoning measures how well you can manipulate an object in your
mind — rotating, folding, or viewing it from another angle — without
physically touching anything. Here are the three formats you'll run into
most often.

## 1. Mental rotation

You're shown an object and several candidates, and asked which candidate
is the *same* object rotated, versus a mirror-image look-alike that only
looks similar. The trap in mirror-image distractors is that they match
the original from most angles — you have to track a specific asymmetric
feature (a notch, a colored face) through the rotation to tell them
apart.

## 2. Cube nets

A net is a flat, unfolded pattern of six squares that folds into a cube.
You're shown a net with symbols on each face and asked which assembled
cube it could produce. The key technique: pick two adjacent squares in
the net and work out which edges touch once folded — most people find it
easier to track two faces at a time than to visualize the whole cube at
once.

## 3. Folding and paper-cutting

You're shown a piece of paper folded one or more times, with a shape cut
out of the folded stack, and asked to predict the pattern of holes when
unfolded. Each fold doubles (or otherwise multiplies) the number of holes
by reflecting the cut across the fold line — working backward one fold at
a time, rather than trying to picture the whole unfolding at once, is the
reliable approach.

## Why this is a distinct skill

People who are strong verbally or numerically aren't automatically strong
spatially, and vice versa — spatial ability loads onto a separate factor
in cognitive-ability research, which is part of why standardized tests
measure it as its own subtest rather than folding it into general
reasoning.
