"IQ" stands for Intelligence Quotient — a single number meant to summarize
performance across a battery of tasks covering reasoning, memory, and
problem-solving, measured relative to other people of a similar age. The
"quotient" in the name is historical: early tests literally divided a
person's "mental age" (based on which age-group's questions they could
answer) by their actual age and multiplied by 100. Modern tests don't use
that formula anymore, but the name stuck.

## Where the idea came from

The idea that a single underlying factor explains why people who do well
on one kind of reasoning task also tend to do well on very different ones
was first proposed by the psychologist Charles Spearman in 1904. He called
that common factor "g," for general intelligence (Spearman, 1904). Later
researchers — notably Raymond Cattell and John Horn — split "g" into
more specific components, like fluid reasoning (solving novel problems)
and crystallized knowledge (accumulated facts and vocabulary), which
eventually merged into what's now called Cattell–Horn–Carroll (CHC)
theory, the framework most modern cognitive tests are built around.

## What a score is actually built from

A real IQ test isn't one type of question repeated — it's a battery
covering several distinct cognitive domains (verbal comprehension, working
memory, processing speed, visual-spatial reasoning, and so on), each
scored separately before being combined into a composite number. That
composite is then compared against a large sample of people who already
took the same test, so the final score tells you where you fall relative
to that group — not an absolute measure of "how smart" someone is in any
context-free sense.

That comparison step — norming against a real population sample — is the
part that takes the most work to do honestly, and it's also the part most
informal "IQ tests" online skip entirely.

## What it doesn't tell you

A cognitive test score says nothing about creativity, emotional
awareness, practical judgment, motivation, or any of the dozen other
things people colloquially mean when they call someone "smart." It also
isn't a fixed, permanent number — performance can shift with sleep,
stress, practice effects, and the specific test used. Treat any single
number, including the one you'll get from an assessment on this site, as
one data point, not a verdict.
