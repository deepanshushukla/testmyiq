If you've searched for this question, you've probably already noticed
that most sites offering a free online IQ test don't answer it honestly.
Here's a straight answer, including about this site's own test.

## What a proctored, clinical test controls for that an online test can't

A psychologist administering a real IQ test in person controls several
things an unsupervised online test simply cannot: confirming it's really
you taking it, preventing lookups or a second person helping, observing
behavior that affects interpretation (fatigue, anxiety, confusion about
instructions), and standardizing the environment (quiet room, no time
pressure from external distractions beyond the test's own timer). Remove
any of those controls and the result becomes noisier — not meaningless,
but less precise than a clinical score.

## What a norming sample actually buys you

Separately from supervision, a clinically validated test is normed
against a large, demographically representative sample (see our article
on <Link href="/blog/how-is-iq-measured">how IQ is measured</Link>). An
online test that skips norming can still measure *something* real — how
many reasoning questions you got right, how that compares to the
difficulty-weighting the test designer chose — but it cannot honestly
claim to place you on a population-referenced percentile, because no
such population exists behind it.

## What this means for a specific number you see online

A raw score or percentile from an unproctored, unnormed online test
should be read as "a rough signal of how you did on this particular set
of questions," not as a clinically equivalent IQ. That's true of most
free online tests, and it's true of ours — which is exactly why we report
an **Estimated Cognitive Performance Score** instead of "IQ," and explain
<Link href="/how-iq-is-calculated">precisely what goes into it</Link>
rather than implying a precision we can't back up.
