"Cognitive ability" isn't one skill — it's a bundle of related but
distinct reasoning abilities. Here's what each category in a full
assessment is actually trying to capture, using the 10 categories our own
assessment scores.

## The reasoning categories

- **<Link href="/logical-reasoning-test">Logical reasoning</Link>** —
  drawing valid conclusions from stated premises (syllogisms, conditional
  "if-then" logic).
- **Numerical reasoning** — spotting the relationship between numbers,
  not just calculating quickly (see our{" "}
  <Link href="/numerical-reasoning-test">numerical reasoning page</Link>).
- **<Link href="/abstract-reasoning-test">Abstract reasoning</Link>** —
  inferring a non-verbal transformation rule from shapes or symbols and
  applying it to something new.
- **<Link href="/spatial-reasoning-test">Spatial reasoning</Link>** —
  mentally rotating, folding, or otherwise manipulating objects in three
  dimensions.
- **<Link href="/pattern-recognition-test">Pattern recognition</Link>** —
  detecting structure in a grid or sequence, often combining two rules at
  once.
- **Matrix reasoning** — a specific, grid-based form of pattern
  recognition (see our{" "}
  <Link href="/blog/matrix-reasoning-questions">matrix reasoning
  article</Link>).
- **Deductive reasoning** — reasoning from general rules to specific
  conclusions, closely related to logical reasoning but focused on
  rule-application rather than syllogistic structure.
- **Visual reasoning** — interpreting and reasoning about visual
  information broadly, beyond pure shape-transformation.
- **Sequence completion** — extending a numeric or symbolic sequence (see
  our{" "}
  <Link href="/blog/number-sequence-puzzles">number sequence article</Link>).
- **Memory and attention** — holding and recalling information after a
  delay, tested with a "memorize now, answer later" format rather than
  showing the answer and the question together.

## Why test all 10 instead of one

Someone strong in logical reasoning isn't automatically strong spatially,
and someone with excellent working memory isn't automatically fast at
numerical pattern-spotting — these are measurably distinct abilities, which
is exactly why a single "IQ" number, built from only one type of
question, would hide more than it reveals. A category-by-category
breakdown tells you something a single composite score can't: where your
own relative strengths are.
